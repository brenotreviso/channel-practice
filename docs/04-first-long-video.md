# 4 · Your first long video

> **In one sentence:** decide what the viewer will be able to do at the end, give them the answer early, write it as you speak, and cut everything else.

This chapter leans on one real creator’s published method: **Internet Shaquille**, a home-cooking YouTuber whose lesson on making instructional videos is public on his second channel ([“How to Make an Internet Shaquille Video”, 16 Jan 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aBUy0z5HNlY); his [official site](https://internetshaquille.com/) links that channel). Where we quote his views, they are his practice, not YouTube rules.

## New words

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Learner outcome** | What the viewer can do after watching. “After this video, you will be able to…” |
| **Prerequisite knowledge** | What you assume they already know, so you do not stop to explain it. |
| **Tension** | The question the title and thumbnail raise. **Resolving** it means answering. |
| **Chapters** | Timestamped sections of a video, set in the description ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9884579?hl=en)). |
| **End screen** | Clickable elements in the last 5–20 seconds of a video ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789?hl=en)). |

## Plan: five questions before you write

From Internet Shaquille’s video, in order:

1. **Who is the learner?** Their fears, biases and goals. He warns that “my audience is everybody” pushes you towards a style built for everyone, which may not be yours.
2. **What is the learner outcome?** Finish the sentence. Tie every part of the video to it.
3. **What do they actually need to learn?** He calls out cooking videos that layer in side facts the viewer does not need.
4. **What tone?** Casual, excited, calm. Choose on purpose.
5. **What can I assume they know?** His example: if a recipe needs minced garlic, he does not stop to teach mincing. Explaining every basic step can make a viewer feel it is too hard.

Write the answers on the [video plan](templates/video-plan.md).

## Write: a script, in your own voice

He is direct about this: write a script every time, then edit it “ruthlessly”. Write the way you speak (“gonna” is fine if you say it), and read it aloud several times at normal pace. Reading aloud is where you hear the repeated word and the sentence that does not need to be there. On each read, cut anything that does not serve the learner outcome.

**Resolve the tension early.** His point: the title and thumbnail already raised the question, so the viewer clicked hoping for an answer. Many videos spend a large share of their time building up to it. In his hummus video he gives three methods early, then builds new tension with a counter-argument (“but what about people who buy it?”) and resolves that too.

**Say hello once.** He keeps a playlist he calls “saying hi twice” for videos that introduce themselves several times before starting. He does not use a title sequence or a catchphrase, and says so as a personal belief.

**Spend real time on the ending.** He estimates he spends about half his writing time on the material and about 40% working out the ending, so the viewer feels they finished something. That is his estimate, not a measurement.

The [long-video script template](templates/long-video-script.md) turns this into a fill-in page.

## A simple shape for a 4–6 minute video

| Part | Rough length | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Open | 0:00–0:30 | Say what they will get and start giving it. No channel intro. |
| Part 1 | | First step or idea, shown, not just said |
| Part 2 | | Second step |
| Part 3 | | Third step, or the “but what about…” |
| Ending | last 20–30 s | What they can now do; one next video (end screen) |

The 4–6 minute range and the timings are **house rules** for a beginner. YouTube does not publish an ideal length. What it does show you is how many people are still watching at 30 seconds, and it suggests revising the title, thumbnail or first 30 seconds if that number is low ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)).

## Film: show it, and get the sound right

- **Show what you are doing.** Internet Shaquille’s example is a how-to where the presenter put the phone down to use both hands, so nothing could be seen.
- **Use the same word for the same thing** every time. A script makes that easy to check.
- **Sound first.** His advice is that a microphone does more for your video than a new lens or camera; he says sound matters far more than picture. That is his strong opinion, not a measured figure, but a cheap clip-on mic is a low-risk first purchase.
- Shoot horizontal (16:9) for long-form. Leave the last 20 seconds clear enough for an end screen ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789?hl=en)).

## Edit and finish

- Cut dead air, false starts and any second introduction.
- Add **chapters** if the video has clear parts: timestamps in the description, the first at 00:00, at least three in order, each at least 10 seconds long ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9884579?hl=en)).
- Add an **end screen** pointing to one related video of yours. It needs a video of at least 25 seconds; up to four elements in the last 5–20 seconds ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789?hl=en)).
- Videos over 15 minutes need a verified account ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/71673?hl=en)). A first video should not need that.

## A generous call to action

Internet Shaquille separates asks that are for you (“help me hit 100,000 subscribers”) from asks that also serve the viewer, such as inviting them to say in the comments what you missed. Both are allowed. Be honest with yourself about which one you are making.

## Maya

Maya’s learner: tired, can cook a little, embarrassed by a bitter dinner. Outcome: “cook the one-pan tomato dinner without the bitter taste.” Assumed knowledge: chopping an onion. Her first draft spends 90 seconds on her day before the pan appears. Her rewrite opens on the pan: “If your tomato dinner tastes bitter, it’s probably the onions. Here’s the fix, then two ways to make it faster.” She reads it aloud four times and removes the word “basically” six times. She orders a clip-on mic. The ending names one thing to try tonight, and the end screen points to her leftovers video.

Fill in the five planning questions and the script template for your next long video. Read it aloud three times. Cut until the open gets to the answer within 30 seconds.
