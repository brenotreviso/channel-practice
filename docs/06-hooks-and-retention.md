# 6 · Hooks and retention

> **In one sentence:** the hook keeps the title’s promise in the first seconds, and the retention graph shows you, second by second, where the video stopped keeping it.

## New words

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | The opening that tells a viewer they are in the right place and why to stay. |
| **Audience retention** | A graph of how many viewers are still watching at each moment ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)). |
| **Intro (in Studio)** | The share of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds (same page). |
| **Average view duration (AVD)** | Average minutes watched among those who stayed to watch, calculated from engaged views ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en)). |
| **Average percentage viewed** | The same idea as a percentage of the video’s length (same page). |
| **Stayed to watch** | For Shorts: the share of times viewers stayed past the first seconds (same page). |

## Long-form hooks: answer, don’t announce

A hook is not a trailer. For a long video, the strongest hook is usually the start of the answer:

- Restate the promise in one sentence, in the viewer’s words.
- Show the thing (the result, the problem, the before-and-after).
- Start the first step.

YouTube’s retention report says a high Intro percentage can mean the first 30 seconds matched what the title and thumbnail led people to expect. Its suggestions for a low one: change the title and thumbnail to better reflect the video, or change the first 30 seconds ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)). Internet Shaquille’s advice to resolve the title’s question early, and not introduce the video several times, is the same idea from a creator ([chapter 4](04-first-long-video.md)).

## Shorts hooks: the first frame

Shorts start playing as people swipe, so the first frame does a thumbnail’s job.

- In a conversation with YouTube’s Shorts product lead Todd Sherman on YouTube’s Creator Insider channel, Jenny Hoyos says the hook is “literally the first frame”: “I really do think you have one second to hook someone, especially on Shorts.” Her hook starts with a shock, “ideally… a visual shock factor”, then sets up what the video is about and what you will get by the end ([Creator Insider, 9 Dec 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_tWy-_otnUc), at 1:37–2:02; [YouTube Blog summary, 28 Jan 2025](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/)). That is her practice, published on a YouTube channel, not a YouTube rule.
- In an earlier podcast interview on Jay Clouse’s channel (a third-party channel), she says a Short’s hook should be strong enough that “if it could be used as a title and thumbnail on a long form… it’ll work for a short”, and that she wants it **visual**, understandable **without sound**, and simple ([podcast interview with Jay Clouse, 9 Oct 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=As7abwNhG7Y)).
- She describes her budget fast-food series: the restaurant front, the item, and the price, framed the same way each time, so returning viewers recognise it (same interview).
- She says she sketches hooks almost like thumbnails, and started by imitating other creators’ hooks before developing her own.
- She checks scripts with a free readability tool and aims at roughly a fifth-grade reading level. That is **her method**, based on her own analysis; it is not a YouTube guideline, and we have not verified it.
- YouTube’s blog quotes creator Jon Youshaei’s “First Frame Rule”: shoot and edit so “the first frame — literally the first thing viewers see — sets up as much tension in the story as possible” ([YouTube Blog, 24 Jan 2023](https://blog.youtube/culture-and-trends/3-creator-economy-trends-2023/)).
- The YouTube app has a **Get feedback** button when you upload a Short, which analyses the hook, pacing and more ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10059070?hl=en)).

Hoyos also gives her own retention and swipe numbers in the interview. They are self-reported, from a channel getting millions of views per Short at the time. **Do not use them as a benchmark** for a new channel.

## Reading the retention graph

In Studio, open a video’s **Analytics**, then **Overview** or **Engagement**, and find **Audience retention**. Data takes 1–2 days to process ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)).

| Shape | What YouTube says it means | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| **Flat** | Viewers watch that part from start to finish | Keep doing this |
| **Gradual decline** | Interest fades over time; most videos taper | Normal. Tighten long middles |
| **Spike** | People rewatched or shared that part, or it was unclear and they replayed it | Check which. Make clear parts clearer; make more of the good parts |
| **Dip** | People skipped or left at that moment | Watch that moment. Cut it, move it, or fix it |

Studio highlights these key moments only when a video is at least 60 seconds long and has at least 100 views (same page). Below that, write a plain honest note instead. You can also compare new and returning viewers’ retention, though some segments need Advanced Mode (same page).

YouTube suggests that if your best moments come late, try introducing them earlier, and consider new videos that expand on your top moments (same page).

## The middle of a long video

Common reasons people leave in the middle (house rules from editing experience, not platform data):

- A section repeats what was already shown.
- A tangent that does not serve the promise.
- Talking about something without showing it.
- A long pause while something cooks, uploads or dries. Cut to the result.

## Maya

Her first long video has a cliff at 0:40, near the end of her 45-second intro (“Hi, I’m Maya, welcome to my channel, so this week has been…”), before the pan is on screen. After she cuts that and opens on the pan for her next video, the early drop is gentler. The first Short, of the low-heat trick, opens on a talking line: “Okay so this is about onions.” It gets swiped. Her next one opens on the two spoons of onions side by side with “3 min vs 12 min” written above. Same video, better first frame.

## Exercise

Open the retention graph for your most recent video (or write a note if it is too new). Find the first dip. Watch that moment. Write one sentence on why someone left, and one change for the next video.
