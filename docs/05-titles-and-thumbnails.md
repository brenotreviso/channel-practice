# 5 · Titles and thumbnails

> **In one sentence:** the title and thumbnail make a promise to someone who has never heard of you; make it specific, make it enticing, and make sure the video keeps it.

## New words

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Packaging** | Title plus thumbnail, together. |
| **Click-through rate (CTR)** | Of the times your thumbnail was shown on YouTube, the share that became a view ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en)). |
| **Clickbait** | Packaging that wins the click and then disappoints. |
| **A/B test** | Showing different versions to different viewers at the same time, to compare them. |

## What YouTube says

From [Thumbnail & title tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en):

- **Be accurate.** If the title does not represent the video, viewers may stop watching, “which can impact discoverability.”
- **Be succinct.** People may see only part of the title. Put the important words first; branding and episode numbers at the end.
- **Limit ALL CAPS and emoji.**
- Thumbnails: think about who the video is for, keep the design simple, use readable text if you use any, and make the image as large as possible. Experiment with updating older thumbnails.
- Take pictures during the shoot so you have thumbnail options.
- The same page states that “90% of the best-performing videos have custom thumbnails.” YouTube does not give the method behind that figure, so read it as YouTube’s own claim.

Hard limits: titles up to **100 characters**, descriptions up to **5,000** ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/57407?hl=en)).

## Honest and enticing are not opposites

Derek Muller of Veritasium made a whole video about this ([“Clickbait is Unreasonably Effective”, 17 Aug 2021](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S2xHZPH5Sng)). Points from it, in his words where quoted:

- He tells of a video he titled “Strange Applications of the Magnus Effect”. Someone else re-uploaded it as “Basketball Dropped From Dam” and, he says, got tens of millions of views on YouTube. His title was accurate but meant little to anyone who did not already know the term.
- He separates two things people call clickbait. Packaging that attracts attention and is true, which a fellow YouTuber suggested calling **“legitbait”**, and packaging that misleads or withholds to trap a click (**“clicktrap”**).
- He says that making the title and thumbnail is “at least half the job,” because better packaging gets more impressions as well as more clicks.
- There is “no one true title and thumbnail” for a video. Each could have many honest versions.
- He describes changing a video’s title and thumbnail after release and seeing it reach more people, while still describing the video accurately.

That video is from 2021 and describes his channel, which is far larger than a beginner’s. Take the idea, not the numbers.

**The test for a beginner:** would someone who clicked, watched to the end, and then reread the title feel it was fair? If yes, make it as enticing as you can.

## Searchable or intriguing

YouTube’s tips name two types: **searchable** titles that say plainly what to expect, and **intriguing** titles that spark curiosity for people who were not looking ([Thumbnail & title tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en)). New channels with no audience usually get found through search first, so searchable is a good default. Muller makes the case for curiosity when the plain name of the topic means nothing to most people (his example is the Collatz conjecture, titled “The Simplest Math Problem No One Can Solve”).

## Thumbnails: the practical bits

- Custom thumbnails need a verified account ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72431?hl=en)).
- Recommended size: **3840 × 2160** for videos, at least 640 pixels wide; 16:9. For Shorts, 2160 × 3840 (9:16), added in Studio on a computer (same page).
- Thumbnails must follow the Community Guidelines; breaking them can mean a strike (same page).
- One subject, readable at phone size, a few words at most. The words should add to the title, not repeat it. (House rules.)

## The description and hashtags

- The first few lines are what people see before “more”, so use them to say what the video is ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12948449?hl=en)).
- Hashtags: up to three of the ones in your description may appear by the title. More than 60 and YouTube ignores all of them; misleading hashtags can get a video removed ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6390658?hl=en)). Two or three accurate ones are plenty.

## Testing titles and thumbnails

YouTube Studio can A/B test up to three titles, thumbnails, or pairs on one long video ([A/B test titles & thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714?hl=en)):

- It needs **advanced features**, a computer, and a long video that is not made for kids, private, or for mature audiences. Shorts cannot be tested.
- The winner is chosen by **watch time share**, not CTR. YouTube’s reason: good packaging helps viewers not “waste their time clicking on the wrong videos.”
- A test can take from a few days to two weeks. “Performed same” and “inconclusive” are common, especially with few impressions.

Without the tool, YouTube advises against swapping several thumbnails on one video and reading the CTR change, because the audience and traffic sources shift ([Impressions & CTR FAQs](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154?hl=en)). For a new channel with small numbers, the simplest test is the next video.

## Maya

Her first title was “My tomato pan.” Accurate, but nobody searches for it. She tries:

- *Why your tomato dinner tastes bitter (it’s the onions)*
- *Fix a bitter tomato sauce: one pan, ten minutes*

She picks the first, because it is the question her comments asked. The thumbnail is her own photo: two spoons of onions side by side, pale and brown, with the words “3 MIN vs 12 MIN”. The video shows exactly that comparison at 1:10, so the promise is kept.
